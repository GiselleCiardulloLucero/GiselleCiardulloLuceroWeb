ARTIST STATEMENT

PROJECT P

Giselle Ciardullo Lucero

English version translated from the Spanish, including the quotations.

While searching for images for another project, I came across photographs and documentary records of women accused of “horizontal collaboration” in France in the aftermath of the Nazi occupation. They were subjected to summary trials, public punishment, partial stripping and the forced shaving of their heads as a mark of dishonour. Many were prostitutes; others had entered into relationships with Nazi soldiers in exchange for food, or had formed emotional attachments to them.

As the search continued, police identification photographs—mugshots—from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries began to emerge from different parts of the world, alongside police archives, records from women’s residential institutions and welfare organisations, contemporary press publications and old photographic records of people of the period, including formerly enslaved women. Traces of anonymous women crossing the barrier of time within a rectangle in which they still seem alive; an institutional record as the sole testimony to their existence. This project begins with them.

United by a shared condition: poverty, precarity, marginalisation. Most are anonymous and forgotten, visual remnants of lives that have reached us only in fragments. Each image belongs to another time, yet continues to act upon the present.

Referring to the archives of the slave trade, the American historian and literary theorist Saidiya Hartman writes: “The archive is, in this case, a death sentence, a grave, the display of a violated body, an inventory of property, a medical treatise on gonorrhoea, a few lines about the life of a whore, an asterisk in the grand narrative of history. For this reason, ‘it is undoubtedly impossible ever to recapture [these lives] in their own right, in the form they might have taken in a state of freedom.’”

Photography separates them from their context, yet the record of their faces inevitably reveals a shared condition and an ordering within a social structure.

They do not conform to ideals of beauty, nor do they wear the clothing of the bourgeoisie. Society regards them with contempt, pity or indifference, as it might an insect or a moth. Ultimately, they are those grey, brown and shadowy moths that beat against a windowpane in search of light.

Looking at them is not a neutral act: the perception of their faces carries a subjective and emotional weight. Pausing to observe them in order to draw and paint them allows me to emphasise the burden of marginalisation and its violence, the marks of exhaustion and the historical indifference weighing upon their vulnerable bodies. Drawing them means looking deeply at them, trying to understand what lies behind these anonymous figures. Portraying the violence inflicted on those who subsist at the margins.

In this work, I have no intention of faithfully copying the photograph. I seek to retain its expressive force, removing the image from its context and giving it a strident colour that contrasts violently with the blacks and whites of the drawing. To move away from an idealised, pitying view of these figures; to approach them as starkly as possible, without beautifying them. In a deliberate effort, I seek to distance myself from ornamental idealisation as much as from infantilised representations and the caricature of the witch or the grotesque figure.
